David, You're Missing a Dimension

A response to "A Few AI Implications We Might Want to Think About"

Aaron Hightower  ·  ahightower.com  ·  Independent Developer & Creative Director

David, your piece is careful and honest — qualities that earned my respect when we worked together on the NanoTech research, and qualities on full display here. But I think you are modeling a single dimension of a multi-dimensional problem and concluding that the dimension you can see is the only one that matters. That is a hyper-generalization, and it leads you toward a false equivalence that the rest of your argument depends on without ever quite stating it outright: that AI-assisted is the same category as AI-authored. It isn't. And the difference is not a technicality.

"The tool doesn't determine the authenticity of the intent behind it. A skilled operator becomes more identifiable through AI, not less — because they are imposing their fingerprint on the output, not accepting the model's default."

Let me come at this from a direction you might not expect. I ride wheelies. Not as a hobby — as a thinking tool. The counterintuitive thing about sustaining a wheelie is that full throttle drops the front wheel back down. Control comes from backing off at precisely the right moment. The skill is living on the curve between too much and too little, where a fractional modulation of a single input — rate, not direction — gives you complete positional authority over a system that looks, from the outside, impossibly complex. You steer left and right not by turning the bars but by understanding the angular momentum state of the machine and adjusting when you apply power. One dimension. Hyperbolic leverage.

The Federal Reserve operates on the same principle. The most watched, most analyzed macroeconomic control system on the planet moves one number. Not a portfolio of interventions — a rate. And the error mode is perfectly symmetric: move too early and you suppress growth unnecessarily; move too late and inflation embeds. Catastrophic outcomes on both sides of a single asymptote, controlled entirely by timing. The complexity of the global economy does not require a complex control surface. It requires one variable, understood deeply, moved at the right moment.

Our NanoTech work pointed at the same truth from a different angle. The skill-game player isn't changing the house's aggregate math. Across all players the edge stays intact. What the skilled player does is exploit a rate differential — a timing advantage in their interaction with the machine — that shifts their personal expected value into positive territory without disturbing the system mean. One dimension. One lever. Asymmetric individual result from a symmetric population outcome. That's not a loophole. That's the physics of skilled interaction with a stochastic system, and it's why we spent so much time on cryptographic score verification: because the math, not the operator, had to be the trusted party.

· · ·

Now here is where I think this lands directly on your argument. You write that AI comes between people, that it softens language and conceals what we really want. You are describing a failure mode of unskilled operators — people who hand the throttle to the machine and walk away. That failure mode is real. But you are generalizing it to the entire class of human-AI interaction, which is the hyper-generalization fallacy. And from that generalization you conclude that AI-assisted communication is equivalent to AI-authored communication, which is the false equivalence.

Consider what I actually do when I produce work with AI. I have spent years developing what I think of as a vocabulary fingerprint — a frequency distribution shaped by Roger Waters, by Krautrock, by three decades of game development, by the specific cognitive texture of living in the Tower District of Fresno. Linguists call this a Zipf profile: the ranked frequency of your word choices, which is forensically individual in the same way a voiceprint is individual. My Zipf fingerprint is not the model's default output distribution. It is mine. When I work with AI, I am imposing that fingerprint on the output, not accepting the model's. The discipline required to do that — to hold the machine to your frequency, your register, your cadence — is not less cognitive work than writing unassisted. In many sessions it is more, because you are simultaneously authoring and editing against a system that wants to regress to its mean.

This is where the Hightower Derivative comes in — a framework I published earlier this year describing AI's effect on cognition as second-order rather than first-order. AI doesn't displace thinking. It changes the rate of change of cognitive output. And just like the wheelie, just like the Fed funds rate, just like the skill-game EV calculation — the leverage lives in controlling that rate, not in maximizing throughput. The people most at risk of the over-reliance you describe are not people who learned to modulate the throttle. They are people who never developed rate-sensitivity in any domain and are now encountering a system that will happily run at full speed forever if you let it. AI didn't create that vulnerability. It exposes it.

"Your filters — friction, struggle, raw unmediated expression — are not what produce authenticity. Frequency distribution is. And frequency distribution survives the tool."

The alternative you offer — thinking for oneself, your admittedly radical departure from current groupthink — I am not against it. I am against the implicit claim that using a tool forfeits that. Pinball has two buttons. Two. The entire game lives inside that constraint. People who understand timing own the table. People who mash the flippers wonder why the ball drains. Same buttons. Different operators. The tool is not the variable.

What I do think you are right about, and what I want to say clearly: the failure mode you are describing is common, it is accelerating, and it is worth the alarm you are raising. People who outsource their expression entirely — who let the model choose not just the words but the intent — are doing something that should trouble us. I agree. But the corrective is not to distrust the tool. The corrective is to develop the rate-sensitivity to know when to pull back. To know when to sit with an incomplete thought. To know when the friction is the point.

You asked what the student is contributing when AI brainstorms, drafts, and polishes. That is the right question. The answer is: it depends entirely on whether the student understands the balance point. If they do, they are contributing authorship — the fingerprint, the intent, the judgment about when to accelerate and when to back off. If they don't, they are just pushing the button and hoping the machine lands it. The machine can tell the difference, even when the instructor cannot. And eventually, so can the reader.

This letter was produced with AI assistance. I wrote every idea in it. I impose my own frequency distribution on every session. I publish that fact as a matter of policy, because I think concealing the mechanism while claiming the output is the one form of AI use that actually deserves the name dishonest. The math — the fingerprint, the Zipf distribution, the authorial intent — is the trusted party. Not the tool. Not the operator's reputation. The math.

Until next time, David. Stay in the dimension where the leverage lives.
