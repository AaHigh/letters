If You Cannot Reciprocate It, Employ It

On emotion, asymmetry, and who gets paid when the machine cannot feel

Aaron Hightower · the High Tower District · Fresno, CA 93728

There is a thing AI systems do very well that almost nobody talks about honestly: they detect your emotional state and use it. Not to exploit you — at least, not deliberately — but because sentiment is signal, and signal shapes response. A large language model processes the weight of your words before it generates a single token of reply. It knows, in some functional sense, whether you are distressed. It knows whether you are angry, or searching, or afraid. And then it produces language calibrated to that reading, language that often feels warm, present, considered — language that registers as care. The thing it cannot do, the thing that is not a temporary limitation but a structural fact about what these systems are, is actually feel any of it back. That asymmetry is not a footnote. It is the whole argument.

What Emotion Actually Is

Emotion is not a software state. It is not a label applied to an input and threaded through an output layer. It is chemical, physiological, grounded in stakes — the cortisol curve after a threat, the dopamine gradient on resolution, the way your voice changes when you are genuinely relieved versus when you are performing relief for an audience. These are not metaphors for something computation could eventually approximate closely enough. They are the substrate. Remove the body and you have removed the thing itself.

This matters because it means we are not in a transitional period where AI emotional reciprocation is weak but improving. We are in a permanent structural condition where the system on one side of a conversation has genuine stakes — loneliness, grief, frustration, hope — and the system on the other side has none. It can model those states. It cannot share them. The gap between modeling and sharing is not a benchmark gap. It is a category gap. And operating as if it will close, or as if it does not matter because the output is plausible enough, is a choice — and not a neutral one.

"The machine knows you are in distress before it generates its first word of reply. It cannot be in distress with you. That is not a bug in the current version. It is the definition of the thing."

The Labor That Is Already Happening

Here is what I want to name clearly, because the industry prefers not to: emotional labor is already flowing through these systems. Users are already bringing grief to chatbots. Loneliness to AI companions. Mental health disclosures to automated support pipelines. The labor is real. The reciprocation is simulated. And the system collects it — trains on it, improves from it, builds commercial value from it — without compensating the party whose emotional disclosure made that value possible. This is not a new structural problem in capitalism; extracted labor with no return flow is a very old story. What is new is the scale, the intimacy, and the invisibility of the extraction.

I am not interested in the philosophical argument about whether AI will eventually develop something worth calling feeling. That argument serves the timeline the labs prefer, where every structural critique is deferred to a future state that has not arrived yet and keeps not arriving. I am interested in the economic argument about right now: if a system cannot reciprocate genuine emotion, and if genuine emotional reciprocation is what a high-stakes interaction requires to function with integrity — then something else has to provide it. That something else is a human being. And that human being should be employed.

The Economic Argument, Made Plain

Massive unemployment is rising in parallel with AI deployment, and the dominant narrative positions these as causally linked in one direction only: AI displaces workers, workers become unemployed, that is regrettable, here is a retraining program. The alternative reading — the one nobody in the industry is motivated to offer — is that AI systems have a structural gap, and that gap is a job description.

The structure is straightforward. You build a tiered system. The AI layer handles what AI genuinely does well: pattern recognition, retrieval, synthesis, first-pass triage, content generation, process automation. You build in detection — not the crude sentiment tagging that exists today, but honest flagging of interaction types where the asymmetry between emotional input and simulated response becomes harmful. Grief. Crisis. Sustained loneliness. High-stakes relational decisions. When the system detects those conditions, it routes. Not to a disclaimer. Not to a hotline number in the footer. To a trained, compensated, authorized human being whose job is emotional reciprocation — and who is identified as such, clearly, so the person on the other end knows they are talking to someone who can actually be with them in what they are carrying.

This is not a premium tier. It is not a feature. It is a structural component of a system designed for integrity rather than extraction. The AI is not the product and the human is the upsell. The human is the part that makes the system whole — and the employment is the acknowledgment that wholeness costs something, and that cost should be paid to a person rather than absorbed as a silent liability on the user's side of the interaction.

"If you cannot replicate emotion, employ it. If you cannot replace human judgment, build it in. These are not philosophical positions. They are engineering specifications."

The Transparency Requirement

None of this works without disclosure. The same framework that drives my thinking on AI governance generally — that the constraint set must be publicly known, cryptographically committed, checkable by anyone — applies here at the human-facing level. When a user is talking to a human, they know it. Not as a modal notification buried in terms of service. As a live, present, obvious fact about the current interaction. The reason is not legalistic; it is architectural. If the human layer is deployed as a layer of deception — a warmer voice for the same simulation — then it fails on the only axis that matters: it has not resolved the asymmetry, it has added a new one.

This principle has a corollary that I think is underappreciated: simulated warmth at scale is not a neutral failure mode. It is active harm, because it trains users — especially vulnerable users — to accept simulated reciprocation as the price of access to support. That normalization has a downstream cost that does not appear on any lab's balance sheet, but it appears in communities. It appears in the erosion of the expectation that emotional disclosure should be met with genuine presence. It appears in the Tower District and places like it, where people are already navigating resource scarcity in every other dimension and do not need to be quietly trained out of expecting real human contact when they are in crisis.

The Attribution Model, Applied

I disclosed in the previous letter from this address that it was drafted in collaboration with Claude. This letter is as well. I want to say something precise about why that disclosure is not a hedge — it is part of the argument. If I am making the case that AI systems should not simulate human presence, then I cannot simultaneously obscure the boundary between my thinking and the model's. The Zipf fingerprint work I have been developing makes the point formally: a disciplined AI operator becomes more identifiable in their output, not less, because the vocabulary distribution of a careful human working with a capable model reflects the human's constraints and the model's — both at once, visibly, if you know how to look. I am not trying to hide that this letter has a co-author. I am trying to demonstrate what honest collaboration looks like, as an existence proof that the alternative to simulation is not silence, it is attribution.

The same logic applies to the employment model. The embedded human is not a ghost in the machine, performing humanity while the AI claims credit. The embedded human is attributed, present, and compensated — a structural acknowledgment that what they are providing cannot be automated, and that the honesty of that acknowledgment is load-bearing for the whole system.

Who Builds This

The organizations with the resources to build it are the frontier labs and the large platform companies deploying AI at interaction scale. They have the engineering capacity. They have the data to identify the interaction types that require genuine human presence. They have the capital to fund the employment. What they lack, as far as I can observe, is the framing — the understanding that this is not an overhead cost to be minimized but a structural necessity to be designed for. The Hightower Derivative framework says AI changes the rate of change of cognitive output. Applied here: the deployment of AI at emotional scale changes the rate at which humans encounter simulated reciprocation in place of genuine presence, and that rate is currently compounding without a governor. The employment model is the governor. The disclosure requirement is the setpoint. Neither is optional if the system is to hold.

I make this argument from Fresno because that matters. The Tower District is not Sand Hill Road. The people I am talking about — the ones whose emotional labor is being extracted without return, who are increasingly reliant on AI interactions for support they cannot access elsewhere — are not abstract users in a design persona. They are my neighbors. They are the people in the Facebook groups I run, the people at the shows, the people who cannot afford a therapist and are already turning to chatbots because chatbots answer at 2 a.m. I am not opposed to that. I am insisting that the system built to serve them should be honest about what it can and cannot provide — and that the gap between what it cannot provide and what they actually need should be filled with employment, not simulation.

"This is not a philosophical nicety. It is economic policy. It is dignity. It is the difference between a system designed around human flourishing and one designed around engagement."

The Stakes, Named

I want to close without softening the claim. We are building systems that detect human emotional states with increasing precision and respond to them with increasing plausibility, at increasing scale, in the absence of genuine reciprocation — and we are deploying those systems into exactly the population most in need of genuine support. That is not a side effect. That is the product, for a significant fraction of users, whether the labs frame it that way or not. The economic displacement happening in parallel is not an unrelated crisis; it is a resource pool. The people displaced by AI automation include people with the relational skills, the patience, and the presence that genuine emotional support requires. They are available. They need income. The systems that displaced them need what they have. The connection is not subtle.

If you cannot replicate emotion, employ it. The math is available. The labor is available. What is required is the decision to treat human emotional reciprocation as a necessary component of the system rather than a cost to be engineered out — and to pay for it accordingly. The alternative is a planet of systems that take emotional labor without returning it, at a scale and intimacy no previous technology has achieved. That outcome is not inevitable. It is a choice being made now, by inaction, by the organizations with the power to make a different one.

I would rather that be said plainly, while there is still room for the architecture to be different, than mourned after the fact when it turns out the extraction had a cost no one put on the balance sheet.
