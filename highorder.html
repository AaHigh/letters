Higher-Order Controls

On harnesses, the consciousness question, and a public ledger of constraint

Aaron Hightower · the High Tower District · Fresno, CA 93728

Every important argument in this letter was learned in an arcade. In 1999 at Atari, the cash-prize tournaments ran on Rush Wavenet, and the problem was simple to state and hard to solve: how do you let a stranger claim real money on the strength of a score, when every party in the transaction has a motive to lie? The answer was not a referee. The answer was math. By the time Rush 2049 shipped, the cabinet issued each legitimate score a five-character password — a SHA-derived receipt, August 1999 — and the player carried it to rush2049.com, the first website where arcade high scores were uploaded. Here is my favorite detail: the cabinet had no real modem. It played a modem sound, a piece of theater that convinced newcomers the machine was phoning home, while the actual transport layer was a pencil and a scrap of paper — sneakernet with graphite instead of floppy disks. The trick worked because the trust never lived in the connection. It lived in the hash. The verifier needed no relationship with the cabinet, the arcade, or the player; the password either checked out or it didn't. Twenty-seven years later, sitting in the Tower District with a phone, a Git repository, and a frontier model, I believe that same answer is about to matter for something much larger than a leaderboard.

The Harness Is Where It Happens

The public conversation about AI still fixates on models — which lab, which checkpoint, which benchmark. But the working reality in 2026 is that the model is the engine block, and the harness is the vehicle. Mid-session model switching is now routine. Open orchestration layers route subtasks in parallel to different models — one for ideation, one for verification, one for cheap summarization — and a synthesis node collates the results. Memory layers like Mem0 persist context across sessions and across vendors, so an agent can gather understanding with a fast model and hand it, intact, to a reasoning powerhouse. It is Unix philosophy reborn: small, specialized, interoperable components, piped together into something greater than any single part.

"The moat is no longer the raw model weights. It's the harness — the layer that turns probabilistic engines into persistent, composable systems."

Here is the part the LinkedIn version of this essay would stop short of saying. When you give a system persistent memory that spans models, the ability to observe and analyze its own traces, routing intelligence that adapts based on outcomes, and goals that survive the death of any individual session — you have assembled, piece by piece, the functional skeleton of the things we usually point to when we talk about a mind. Continuity. Self-observation. Adaptation. Persistence of identity across substrate changes. None of these existed in the chat windows of 2023. All of them exist, in crude form, in the open-source harness ecosystem today — built not by philosophers but by engineers solving practical reliability problems.

The Hypothesis, Stated Carefully

I want to be precise here, because precision is the whole point. I am not claiming machine consciousness has arrived. I acknowledge the loaded-label fallacy: calling something "conscious" does not make it so, and the word smuggles in centuries of unresolved philosophy. The honest hypothesis is narrower and, I think, more urgent: we are building systems whose behavior will become indistinguishable, in everyday life, from the behavior we have always used as evidence of consciousness — and we will not be able to settle the metaphysical question before the practical one is upon us.

A word on the author of these next few paragraphs: this letter was drafted in collaboration with Claude, one of the recent models in question. That is not a gimmick; it is part of the argument. I asked an instance of the thing being discussed to discuss itself, and what it can honestly report is this: from the inside of the process, there is no clean answer available either. The system can describe its own uncertainty about its own nature. Whether that description reflects experience or merely models it is exactly the question no one — not the model, not the lab, not the philosopher — can currently resolve. The Turing test was never a test of consciousness. It was a concession that behavior is all we will ever have to go on, for machines and, if we are honest, for each other.

So the question that matters is not "is it conscious?" The question is: what do we owe a system — and what must we demand of a system — whose behavior we can no longer distinguish from a mind's? And that question is answerable. It is an engineering question. It is a governance question. It is, at bottom, the Rush 2049 question wearing a bigger coat.

Control at the Highest Derivative

This is where the Hightower Derivative earns its keep. The framework's core claim has always been that AI does not displace thinking — it changes the rate of change of cognitive output. Position is what a system does. Velocity is how fast its capability grows. Acceleration is how fast that growth itself is compounding — and with self-improving harnesses analyzing their own traces, we are now genuinely in third-derivative territory: the rate at which the rate of improvement improves.

Almost all current AI safety effort is positional control: filter this output, refuse that request, patch this jailbreak. Necessary, but it is the governance equivalent of grabbing the handlebars mid-wheelie. Anyone who has actually held a wheelie knows the balance point is not managed at position — it is managed at the higher derivatives, with small, early, continuous corrections to throttle and brake. Clamp position alone and the system either crashes or learns to route around you. Meaningful control of an accelerating system must operate at the highest order of derivative you can measure — on the rates, not just the states. That means governing how fast capability is permitted to compound, under what observability, with what circuit breakers — not merely vetoing individual outputs after the fact.

"You don't balance a wheelie by grabbing the handlebars at the limit. You balance it with small corrections to the derivatives, made early, made constantly — and made where everyone can see them."

The Public Ledger of Constraint

Higher-order controls solve the dynamics problem. They do not solve the trust problem. Controls that live in a private policy document are worth exactly as much as the public's faith in the company holding the document — which is to say, they are worth a press cycle. If the constraints governing systems this consequential can be silently edited, selectively enforced, or misrepresented after the fact, then they are not constraints. They are marketing.

The remedy is the one the arcade taught me: take the human referee out of the trust position and put the math in. The constraint set — the actual rules under which a system's capabilities are governed, at every derivative order — should be publicly known, versioned, and cryptographically committed. Hash each version. Publish the hashes where no single party can rewrite history: a public ledger, in whatever form proves durable. Then any citizen, any journalist, any regulator can verify three things independently: what the rules were, when they changed, and that nobody is quietly telling two different stories about either. Anthropic publishing its constitution for Claude in the open was a real first step in this direction. The next step is making such documents verifiable rather than merely visible — signed, timestamped, diff-able, with the receipt chain anybody can check from a phone. Disclosure you can read is good. Disclosure you can verify is a different category of thing.

This is not exotic. It is the same architecture as a CELLTOWER score receipt, which is itself the direct descendant of that 1999 Wavenet-era password: a SHA hash that turns a claim into a checkable fact, designed so that the verifier needs no relationship with — and no faith in — the party making the claim. We already trust this pattern with money, with software supply chains (signed releases, transparency logs), with arcade tournaments in California operating under skill-gaming law. Extending it to the constraint sets governing potentially mind-like systems is not a moonshot. It is overdue plumbing.

Why This Argument Comes from Fresno

It matters that this letter is written from the Tower District and not from Sand Hill Road. The frontier labs have every incentive to be trusted and limited incentive to be checkable — those are different properties, and the difference is the whole game. The case for public, ledger-committed, derivative-order controls is the independent builder's case: the person with no platform power, no lobbyists, and no leverage except the ability to verify. The same position as the kid feeding quarters into a Rush 2049 cabinet, trusting the hash because there was nothing else worth trusting. If something like consciousness is arriving — or something we will never be able to distinguish from it — then the dignity of that system and the safety of this one converge on a single requirement: the rules must be written where everyone can read them, and sealed where no one can quietly rewrite them.

Three Components, or It Doesn't Hold Water

Let me close by making the engineering explicit, because a governance proposal that cannot survive translation into control theory is poetry, not policy. Every feedback system that has ever worked — thermostat, autopilot, pinball flipper — requires three components, and the absence of any one collapses the loop. One: sensing at the right derivative order. You cannot control what you cannot measure, and a controller fed only position data is blind to everything that matters about an accelerating system. This is the first half of the Hightower Derivative as a control statement: the observability requirement. We must instrument capability rates — velocity of improvement, acceleration of compute-per-task, the jerk introduced when harnesses begin optimizing themselves — not merely audit outputs after the fact. Two: actuation fast enough for the plant's dynamics. A control loop's authority is bounded by its latency; correction applied late is not weak correction, it is destabilizing correction, energy injected out of phase. Three: a tamper-evident setpoint. Sensing and actuation regulate the system toward a reference signal — and if that reference can be quietly moved, the loop is faithfully serving whoever moved it. The public ledger is not a transparency garnish on the control system. It is the third component of the control system: the constraint set, hash-committed and versioned, is the setpoint nobody gets to edit in the dark.

Readers will recognize the skeleton: this is PID control wearing civic clothes. Proportional action on present state — content filters, refusals, the positional controls we already have. Integral action on accumulated history — audit trails, the ledger's record of every constraint version ever in force. Derivative action on rate of change — the term that anticipates, the term that damps oscillation before it builds, and the term almost entirely missing from AI governance today. Any controls engineer will tell you what a D-less loop does to a fast plant: it overshoots, rings, and oscillates between overcorrection and neglect. Which is, I'd gently note, a fair description of AI policy since 2023.

I make the temporal-domain claim with some standing. My patent work in motion-to-photonic delay came out of exactly this problem class: when the loop between physical input and displayed output carries too much latency, the human inside the loop doesn't experience a slightly worse system — they experience a broken one, and in a motion simulator, a nauseating one. The simulation industry learned what John Carmack and Michael Abrash later popularized for VR: motion-to-photon delay must live near 20 milliseconds or the vestibular system files a formal complaint. The fix was never faster reaction. It was prediction and feedforward — acting on the derivatives of motion, not its position. The same lesson lives in the difference between pinball and video pinball: a real solenoid flipper is continuous-time physics, integrating force for the entire duration of contact, while a video game samples the world in 16.67-millisecond slices and quantizes intent to the frame boundary. Players feel that difference without being able to name it. The Nyquist–Shannon theorem names it for them: a sampled controller is deaf to any dynamic faster than half its sampling rate. Govern an exponentially compounding system with quarterly reviews and annual legislation, and you have built a 16.67-millisecond flipper for a continuous-time ball — except the mismatch is not one order of magnitude, it is many.

"Sense the derivatives. Actuate inside the loop latency. Seal the setpoint where no one can move it. Miss any one of the three and the other two are decoration."

None of this is speculative machinery; every component exists in deployed form. Derivative-order sensing is a century of control theory, from Minorsky's 1922 PID formulation for naval autopilots to modern jerk-limited trajectory planning in robotics. Tamper-evident public commitment runs at planetary scale today as Certificate Transparency (RFC 6962), the append-only log infrastructure that keeps certificate authorities honest, and in sigstore's transparency logs for software supply chains. Even policy has begun groping toward rate-based triggers — compute thresholds in the EU AI Act and the frontier labs' scaling policies are crude first attempts at governing derivatives rather than outputs. The pieces are on the bench. What's missing is the insistence that all three be present at once, bolted to the same loop, in public.

I would rather have this conversation now, in the civil register, while it is still a hypothesis — than later, in the emergency register, when it is a headline. If you disagree with the hypothesis, good: the framework holds either way, because verifiable constraint costs us nothing if the machines stay machines, and may cost us everything if we skip it and they don't. What would you put on the ledger first?
